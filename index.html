Abstract

SLAMS “is a method used for autonomous vehicles that lets you build a map and localize your vehicle in that map at the same time.” (MathWorks (n.d.)) This technology/ algorithm is used in applications that require the vehicle to map out unknown environments. SLAM’s plays major roles in critical systems/ situations where it is not feasible for humans to take part in; but how does it truly work? Autonomous vehicles are equipped with sensors (microphones, camera, temperature sensors). These sensors are used to detect changes in physical conditions, or environmental parameters and converts them into measurable signals. Sensors are essentially “eyes and ears” of autonomous vehicles and other machines (Abi Royen (2025)) (Muhammad Wicaksono and Shin, S.Y. (2025)). With this, the autonomous vehicle can use this data to understand the surroundings and then make decisions.

Analysis and discussion

With the definition of SLAMS established, we can discuss how these technologies are deployed across a wide range of systems. One use of this technology is in search and rescue, these types of applications perfectly demonstrate the usefulness of this technology, as in situation which emergency services would struggle to help people UAV’s can be used in place of humans (Muhammad Wicaksono and Shin, S.Y. (2025)). One major hurdle that arises in search and rescue operations is time constraints, as when there's an emergency e.g someone stuck or injured, every second matters. UAVs can be used in these operations to scout ahead, allowing the rescuers to plan more efficiently and optimise search coordination by clearing large areas of land to avoid wasting time (Piatt, L. (2023)).

UAV's can be used for emergencies, but those same systems can be used to take you around your city. This technology has already been used in places in North America (about 17 cities); just recently, robotaxis have been granted licences to operate in the UK in the city of London. With the use of advanced AI and sensors, the data from all the sensors, such as proximity sensors and cameras, can be stitched together to make one clear image, which can be processed by the AI to make live decisions. The AI is made from nodes; once you have enough, it forms a neural network.

The use cases for UAVs span much further than search and rescue, robotaxis and other ways we automate our lives. In fact, it stretches far beyond Earth into the cosmos. One example of this is the Ingenuity Mars rover, " the little helicopter that could" (Cameron, A. (2021)); this simple drone was developed by NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory. On paper, the SOC that was controlling most of the heavy tasks was a Snapdragon 810 (Cameron, A. (2021)), while this information by itself is meaningless without context; this SOC was used in smartphones in 2014, 6 years before the launch. The lack of newer specialised hardware was intentional, as the drone was just meant to demonstrate that "aerial scouting and other functions could benefit future exploration of Mars and other worlds"(Cameron, A. (2021)); the drone far exceeded the original goal. Even the sensors used were essentially off the shelf parts, made by brands like Bosch and Garmin (Cameron, A. (2021)). The drone being unmanned was necessary, as data must be sent “over millions of miles”, which could lead to significant delays between inputs (NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL). (2020)) (Cameron, A. (2021)). The autonomous system wasn't just used to control the drone they were also used to control the heating systems to ensure that the systems don't freeze in the harsh conditions of Mars.
